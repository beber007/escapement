# The Raspberry Pi RP2040 port

The Raspberry Pi Pico port lives in `Escapement/CORTEX-Mx/RP2040/` and comes to
**1,245 lines**, headers and linker script included, against 4,359 for the STM32
layer — the latter spends 1,757 lines on its per-derivative vector tables
alone, where the RP2040 has a single part and 26 interrupts.

| File | Role | Lines |
|---|---|---:|
| `Escapement_Timer.c` | the five functions the kernel expects, on TIMER | 246 |
| `Escapement_TimerEvent.c` | event manager on a free alarm, for event-driven tasks | 188 |
| `Escapement_UART.c` | PL011 driver, transmission through a queue of buffers | 222 |
| `Escapement_Processor.c` | clock tree: crystal, PLL, derived clocks; DVFS driver | 230 |
| `Escapement_Interrupts.c` | vector table and dispatch of the 26 IRQs | 83 |
| `RP2040_SRAM.ld` | linker script | 63 |
| `Escapement_RamEntry.S` | entry stub for a firmware loaded into SRAM | 32 |
| headers | | 181 |

## Two design choices

**The firmware executes from SRAM.** The RP2040 normally boots from an external
QSPI flash, which requires a 256-byte second-stage bootloader with its CRC.
Linking into SRAM avoids it — this is what the `no_flash` build type of the SDK
does — and takes the flash out of the power measurement, which will serve the
power-aware variant. The application points `VTOR` at `0x20000000` before
enabling interrupts.

**Time is rebuilt modulo 2³⁰.** The RP2040 counter is a free-running 64-bit one
that never wraps in practice, whereas Escapement expects a counter that wraps at
2³⁰ and signals every wrap. `ALARM0` carries the deadline set by `_OSSetTimer`,
and `ALARM1`, rearmed on each 2³⁰ boundary, plays the part of the overflow
interrupt.

Above all, **the timer is fed by a 1 µs tick derived from `clk_ref`, independent
of `clk_sys`**: changing the core frequency does not move the time base of the
kernel. On the STM32, the timer clock follows the core clock, which complicates
DVFS.

## Execution on real hardware

The port has been **validated on a Raspberry Pi Pico W**, loaded into SRAM over
SWD with the official Debug Probe — no BOOTSEL button needed:

```sh
openocd -f interface/cmsis-dap.cfg -c 'adapter speed 5000' -f target/rp2040.cfg \
        -c 'init; reset halt; load_image build/UARTEchoPico.elf; resume 0x20000000; exit'
```

`UARTEchoPico` sends back every byte received on UART0 (GP0 and GP1, 115200
baud):

```
sent b'escapement'  -> received b'escapement'   ECHO OK
sent b'RP2040 ok'   -> received b'RP2040 ok'    ECHO OK
sent b'0123456789'  -> received b'0123456789'   ECHO OK
```

And `TaskLEDPico`, inspected over SWD while it was running:

```
PC       = 0x20000864     the wfi of IdleTask
timer    advances by 136,241 µs between two reads
alarm0   armed 1,620 µs ahead of the counter
gpio_out = 0x8            GP3 high, a task was executing
```

The scheduler does arm its deadlines in the future.

> On a **Pico W**, the on-board LED is not on GP25: it is driven by the CYW43439
> wireless chip, and GP25 serves as the chip select for that chip.
> `TaskLEDPico` nevertheless declares it as its first output — on a Pico W, look
> at GP2 and GP3 with a scope, or go through the UART.

## Kernel time needs an origin

The RP2040 counter runs freely from power-up and is never reset, whereas
Escapement assumes its clock starts near zero. On a board that has been powered
for a few hours the counter exceeds a billion microseconds: the kernel believed
itself instantly late on every deadline and fell onto its overload guard.

`_OSStartTimer` therefore captures the instant it starts, and every kernel time
is counted from that origin. **This defect was only visible on hardware**: the
counter of the emulator always starts at zero.

## The core runs at 125 MHz

`OSInitializeSystemClocks` engages the PLL, because the per-activation cost of
the kernel sets a floor on the achievable period. The power-aware kernel then
moves between 12, 50 and 125 MHz, see `power-aware.md`. The reference and peripheral
clocks, on the other hand, **stay on the crystal**: the first keeps the
one-microsecond tick exact whatever the core does — precisely what makes this
chip a good target for the power-aware variant — and the second lets the UART
divide a frequency the driver knows.

## Cost of one scheduling round

Measured on the board, from the hardware timer interrupt to the rearming of the
next deadline — which covers the interrupt, the software handler, the transfer
of arrivals into the ready queue and the election of the next task. Task set of
four: 1, 10, 20 and 60 ms, whose arrivals all fall on the millisecond, hence one
round per millisecond. Remeasured on 2026-09-23, 10 s per build, at `-O2`:

| | Rounds in 10 s | Mean | Maximum | Share of the processor |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Hard, earliest-deadline-first (as shipped) | 10,061 | **3.2 µs** | **8 µs** | **0.33 %** |
| Hard, deadline-monotonic | 10,060 | 3.2 µs | 8 µs | 0.33 % |
| Soft | 10,061 | 4.4 µs | 13 µs | 0.44 % |
| Power-aware | 10,060 | 4.2 µs | 9 µs | 0.43 % |

Some 400 cycles per round at 125 MHz for the hard kernel, whichever the
scheduling algorithm: ordering the ready queue by deadline costs nothing
measurable on four tasks. At `-O0` the hard kernel took 7.0 µs on average and
26 µs at worst, so these numbers say as much about the build as about the design.

The first publication gave 3.2 and 8 µs as well, but 13,409 rounds in 10 s and
0.43 % of the processor. Rebuilt from the very revision that published them
(7c84eca, with only the later fix of the pending timer interrupt, without which
it no longer starts after a load), the same image now counts 10,061 rounds: the
cost per round reproduces, the count of rounds did not, and 10,061 is the one
this task set can produce.

The power-aware kernel always runs its rounds at 125 MHz: its idle task sleeps at
full speed (see below), so the alarm finds the core there, and the round lowers
the speed only once the next deadline is armed. The trace of that build, whose
points add about a microsecond, gives 5.3 µs on average and 11 µs at worst over
276 rounds, all begun at 125 MHz.

The instrumentation lives in `Escapement_Timer.c` under
`ESCAPEMENT_MEASURE_SCHEDULING_COST`, and is **off in the example as shipped**:
it adds work to the critical path it measures, and it changes how the board
behaves under a debugger. To reproduce the figures, uncomment it in
`Escapement_Config.h`, rebuild, and run `tools/measure_cost.sh`, which refuses to
run against a binary that carries no counters.

That second effect is worth spelling out. The timer of the RP2040 stops while
**any** core is halted by the debugger, and the probe halts both — it never
resumes core 1, because resuming it from wherever the bootrom left it restarts
the chip. So with `TIMER_DBGPAUSE` at its normal value, a single SWD inspection
freezes the kernel clock for good: the board keeps executing, and its outputs
still move, but no deadline is ever reached again. That is the right behaviour
for stepping through the kernel, and the wrong one for watching it run, which is
why the measurement build clears the register.

One more trap, on the reading side: the counters must be read one symbol at a
time. The linker reorders them between optimisation levels, so reading four
consecutive words returns whatever happens to sit there — in our case
neighbouring pointers, presented as microseconds.

## Confirmed by an independent instrument

The three outputs were measured with the frequency counter of a Bus Pirate v4,
connected to `AUX` and a common ground:

| Output | Task | Expected | Measured | Deviation |
|---|---|---:|---:|---:|
| `GP4` | 1 ms, toggles on every instance | 500.000 Hz | **500.02 Hz** | +40 ppm |
| `GP2` | 20 ms, pulse | 50.0000 Hz | **50.0014 Hz** | +28 ppm |
| `GP3` | 60 ms, pulse of varying width | 16.66667 Hz | **16.66713 Hz** | +28 ppm |

Eight consecutive readings on `GP3` give the same value to within one part in a
hundred thousand. The +28 ppm deviation, **identical on the two most precise
measurements**, is not noise: it is the tolerance of the crystal on the board
relative to the reference of the instrument, squarely within the usual ±30 ppm.

This is the one measurement in the project that depends **neither on the kernel,
nor on the emulator, nor on the debugger**. A second device confirms the three
scheduling periods, including that of the task whose execution time varies from
one instance to the next — its period does not.

## The timer must stop with the debugger

The RP2040 freezes its counter as soon as a core is halted by the debugger. That
behaviour is **kept**, and turning it off cost a long investigation.

Without it, the kernel clock keeps running through the halts that loading over
SWD imposes — reset, image transfer, restart of the second core, several tens of
milliseconds in all. The kernel therefore starts **already that late**. A task of
10 ms or more absorbs it; a task of 1 ms is born twenty periods late, and the
catch-up loop makes it arrive again before it has had a chance to run: the
overload guard fires, rightly so.

The state captured when it fired already said as much, with three tasks late by
18 to 26 ms at the same instant — exactly the duration of the loading sequence.
Debugging a real-time kernel assumes its clock stops with it. Setting
`TIMER_DBGPAUSE` to 0 gives the opposite behaviour, useful for measuring wall
time across a halt.

## Two pitfalls met along the way

**Reloading without resetting.** Loading a firmware over SWD on top of another
leaves the peripherals in the state the previous one left them. The timer stayed
armed, its interrupt fired, and `_OSIOHandler` found no descriptor: it went into
its trap with interrupts disabled. Hence the `reset halt` before `load_image`.

**Priming the transmission.** On the USART of an STM32, `TXE` reflects a state:
arming the interrupt fires it immediately. On the PL011 of the RP2040 it fires
on a FIFO threshold being crossed — arming it while the FIFO is already empty
produces nothing. `OSEnqueueUART` has to write the first bytes itself to prime
the transmission.

## Execution verified under emulation

On an emulated Pico (see `emulation/renode/RP2040.md`), the three periodic tasks
of `TaskLEDPico.c` over one second:

| Output | Period | Instances | Measured ratio | Theoretical ratio |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| GPIO 25 (on-board LED) | 10 ms | 100 | 5.88 | 6.00 |
| GPIO 2 | 20 ms | 50 | 2.94 | 3.00 |
| GPIO 3 | 60 ms | 17 | 1.00 | 1.00 |

The processor ends up in the `wfi` of `IdleTask`, and the emulation reproduces
the ratios later measured on the board.

The emulation now runs in CI on every push (`emulation/renode/RP2040.md`), on the
hard, the soft and the power-aware kernel under both scheduling algorithms, and
the power-aware one undervolted: the 1 ms probe is timed within 2 %, the three
tasks rise and fall, the UART echo answers, the timer events wake their
event-driven tasks on time, scheduling survives the 2^30 wrap of the kernel
clock, and the DVFS driver raises the voltage before the frequency and lowers it
after.

What the board has run is the hard kernel, `TaskLEDPico` and `UARTEchoPico` above,
since 2026-09-22 the power-aware kernel with its DVFS driver, and since 2026-09-23
the timer events under all three kernels — see below. The wrap and undervolting
have been seen under emulation only.

## The power-aware kernel on the board, through a trace

Watching a running board from the debugger turned out to disturb what it watched,
three ways:

- loading an image stops both cores and resumes core 0 only, and the timer, set by
  the port to pause while a core is stopped by the debugger, stays frozen;
- stopping core 0 to read its registers lets time run on while the tasks do not: the
  1 ms probe misses its deadline and the kernel stops on its overload guard — which
  was first taken for a defect of the kernel;
- each read of memory over SWD takes about a millisecond, so a series of them is no
  snapshot.

Hence a trace (`Escapement/CORTEX-Mx/Escapement_Trace.h`, `make TRACE=1`): the
port writes the events of the scheduling, with the time of the timer, into a ring
buffer of 256 entries, and `tools/read_trace.py` suspends the recording, reads the
buffer in one transfer and lets it go on, without stopping a core. Built with
`ESCAPEMENT_MEASURE_SCHEDULING_COST` as well, so that the kernel frees the timer
itself, the image runs untouched from the load on.

The hard kernel, one round of every millisecond:

```
 40000 us  alarm0
 40001 us  soft>                       software timer handler entered
 40005 us  set_timer ahead 996 us      next deadline armed
 40006 us  soft<
 40007 us  mark gpio 4 start           the probe task runs
```

The power-aware kernel, whose first image stalled at once — the trace showed the
alarm and the handler entered, then nothing. The timer, which a reset of the
processors alone does not reset, still had the alarm of the previous image armed;
the NVIC kept its interrupt pending although it was not yet enabled, and took it as
soon as it was, before the idle task had set the origin of time. The port now
clears that pending state. With it, the DVFS driver works on the silicon:

```
 31999 us  alarm0
 32000 us  soft>
 32004 us  set_timer ahead 995 us
 32007 us  speed 125 MHz -> 12 MHz     slowed down for the probe
 32016 us  soft<                        9 us: the handler, now at 12 MHz
 32029 us  mark gpio 4 start
 32047 us  speed 12 MHz -> 125 MHz     back to full speed in the idle task
```

The 50 MHz point is taken too, for the other tasks; the figure in the
README draws such a trace, `tools/read_trace.py --csv` feeding
`tools/dvfs_figure.py`. One thing the trace makes
plain: the idle task goes back to 125 MHz before it sleeps (`_OSSleep` of the
power-aware kernel), so the core spends nearly all its time asleep with the PLL at
full speed. Whether that costs anything is for the current bench to say.

## Timer events on the board

`TestTimerEventPico` leaves a mark in the trace when each of its tasks raises or
lowers its output, and the event manager one when it delivers an event, with how
late the alarm fired; `tools/read_trace.py --pins` sums the marks up per output.
Five seconds of each kernel on 2026-09-23:

| Kernel | GPIO 2: period, high | GPIO 3: period, high |
|---|---|---|
| Hard | 4999–5001 µs, 1012 µs | 10000 µs, 2012 µs |
| Soft | 4998–5002 µs, 1011–1012 µs | 10000 µs, 2012 µs |
| Power-aware | 4999–5001 µs, 1012–1013 µs | 10000 µs, 2013 µs |

Every event was delivered on the microsecond its alarm was set for. The 12 µs over
the requested 1000 and 2000 are the path from the alarm to the event-driven task:
the interrupt, the software timer handler, the switch of context. The power-aware
kernel changed no speed: event-driven tasks keep it at full speed by design.

The frequency counter of the Bus Pirate, on `GP3`, read 100.0031 Hz eight times in
a row under each of the three kernels, against 100 Hz expected: +31 ppm, the
crystal of the board as before, and the same to the last digit whichever kernel
schedules the task and the event that shape the output.

## Where the idle task sleeps

The power-aware kernel lets its idle task sleep at 125 MHz: `_OSSleep` raises the speed
before each `WFI`, and the core spends nearly all its time there. `make KERNEL=PA
SLEEP_SPEED=n` chooses another operating point (0 for 12 MHz, 1 for 50 MHz); the default
is unchanged. Measured on the board on 2026-09-23, `TaskLEDPico` and
`TestTimerEventPico`, the cost of a round with the counters of `measure_cost.sh`, the
rest from the trace:

| Idle sleeps at | Cost of a round | Share of the processor | Alarm to probe | High time of the 1 ms event |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 125 MHz (default) | 4.2 µs, 9 at worst | 0.43 % | 30 µs | 1012 µs, alarm on time |
| 50 MHz | 11.2 µs, 16 at worst | 1.13 % | | |
| 12 MHz | 21.3 µs, 26 at worst | 2.14 % | 57 µs | 1084 µs, alarm 7 µs late |

Every deadline still holds and the periods do not move — the delay is the same at every
round. What sleeping slow costs is latency and overhead: everything before the timer
handler raises the speed runs at 12 MHz — the entry of the interrupt, the alarm handler,
the switch to the software timer, the first part of the kernel's handler — and the
change of speed itself takes several microseconds. The timer events pay most, their
handler never raising the speed. Whether sleeping at 12 MHz saves more than those
microseconds cost is for the current bench to say.
