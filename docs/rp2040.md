# The Raspberry Pi RP2040 port

**In short.** On a Pico, the hard, the soft and the power-aware kernels all run. The
power-aware kernel runs under its four policies. Its DVFS driver moves between 12, 50
and 125 MHz. The timer events, the UART and the 4-slot buffer between the two cores run
as well. The board gave these measurements:

- a scheduling round costs 4.4 µs under the hard kernel. That is 0.44 % of the processor
  at one round a millisecond. Every log the bench has posted on a pull request since
  2026-10-13 reads that figure. It was 3.5 µs until 2026-10-10 (#47, "Cost of one
  scheduling round");
- the periods read +28 ppm on a frequency counter, as a Pico 2's do: the cause is the
  counter's reference, not the board ("The Pico 2 on the board");
- timer events are delivered on the microsecond;
- a change of speed takes 4 to 9 µs;
- the 4-slot buffer carried 326,525 reads between the cores, and none was torn.

The current of this board was measured on 2026-10-10 (`power-aware.md`, "Measuring the
RP2040"). DVFS saves up to some 5 % with the idle task in WFI. It saves nothing once the
idle task sleeps in SLEEP. On the RP2350, DVFS saves some 10 % at best (issue #23). The
undervolted core has run on the board without the kernel (`SleepPico`). Under the
kernel, it has run in emulation only.

The port lives in `Escapement/CORTEX-Mx/RP2040/`. With its headers and linker scripts,
it came to **2,158** lines on 2026-10-10, counted below. The count first written was
2,156: the headers were miscounted by two. The port had 1,768 lines on 2026-10-05,
before the image in flash and the sleep with PLL_SYS stopped were added. The STM32 layer
was removed on 2026-09-26. It had 4,375 lines, and 1,757 of them were its per-derivative
vector tables, but the RP2040 is a single part with 26 interrupts.

| `Escapement_TimerEvent.c` | event manager on a free alarm, for event-driven tasks | 231 |

## Two design choices

The firmware executes from SRAM. The RP2040 normally boots from an external QSPI flash.
That boot needs a 256-byte second-stage bootloader with its CRC. Linking into SRAM
avoids the bootloader, as the `no_flash` build type of the pico-sdk does. It also keeps
the flash out of the power measurement the power-aware kernel will need. The image
begins with a stub that sets the stack pointer (`Escapement_RamEntry.S`). The vector
table follows the stub, 256-byte aligned (`RP2040_SRAM.ld`). The application points
`VTOR` at that table before it enables interrupts.

Time is rebuilt modulo 2^30. The RP2040's counter is a free-running 64-bit counter that
never wraps in practice, but Escapement expects a counter that wraps at 2^30 and signals
each wrap. `ALARM0` carries the deadline set by `_OSSetTimer`. `ALARM1` is rearmed on
each 2^30 boundary and stands in for the overflow interrupt.

The timer counts a 1 µs tick derived from `clk_ref`, not from `clk_sys`. Changing the
core frequency therefore leaves the kernel's time base where it was. On an STM32 the
timer clock follows the core clock, which makes DVFS harder.

## Execution on real hardware

The port first ran on a Raspberry Pi Pico W. The firmware is loaded into SRAM over SWD
through the Raspberry Pi Debug Probe, with no BOOTSEL button to press:

```sh
openocd -f interface/cmsis-dap.cfg -c "$(tools/probe.sh)" -c 'adapter speed 5000' \
        -f target/rp2040.cfg \
        -c 'init; reset halt; load_image build/UARTEchoPico.elf; resume 0x20000000; exit'
```

`tools/probe.sh` selects the Debug Probe by its USB ids and serial. Every tool has done
so since 2026-09-25 (see “Cost of one scheduling round” for the reason).

`UARTEchoPico` sends back every byte it receives on UART0 (GP0 and GP1, 115200 baud):

```
sent b'escapement'  -> received b'escapement'   ECHO OK
sent b'RP2040 ok'   -> received b'RP2040 ok'    ECHO OK
sent b'0123456789'  -> received b'0123456789'   ECHO OK
```

`TaskLEDPico` was inspected over SWD while it ran. It shows the scheduler arming its
deadlines in the future:

```
PC       = 0x20000864     the wfi of IdleTask
timer    advances by 136,241 µs between two reads
alarm0   armed 1,620 µs ahead of the counter
gpio_out = 0x8            GP3 high, a task was executing
```

> On a **Pico W**, the on-board LED is not on GP25. The CYW43439 wireless chip drives it,
> and GP25 is that chip's select line. `TaskLEDPico` still declares GP25 as its first
> output, so on a Pico W watch GP2 and GP3 with a scope, or use the UART.

## Kernel time needs an origin

The RP2040's counter runs from power-up and is never reset, but Escapement assumes its
clock starts near zero. After a few hours on, the counter is past a billion
microseconds. The kernel then found itself late on every deadline at once and fell onto
its overload guard.

`_OSStartTimer` therefore records the instant it starts. Every kernel time counts from
that origin. **Only the hardware showed this defect**: the emulator's counter always
starts at zero.

## The core runs at 125 MHz

`OSInitializeSystemClocks` engages the PLL, because the cost of each activation sets a
floor under the shortest usable period. The power-aware kernel then moves between 12, 50
and 125 MHz (`power-aware.md`). The reference and peripheral clocks **stay on the
crystal**. The reference clock keeps the 1 µs tick exact whatever the core does. The
peripheral clock gives the UART a frequency its driver knows.

## Cost of one scheduling round

The cost is measured on the board from the hardware timer interrupt to the rearming of
the next deadline. That interval covers:

- the interrupt;
- the software handler;
- the move of the arrivals into the ready queue;
- the election of the next task.

The task set has four tasks, of 1, 10, 20 and 60 ms. Their arrivals all fall on the
millisecond, so there is one round per millisecond. The cost was remeasured on
2026-09-23, about 10 s per build, at `-O2`:

| | Rounds (about 10 s) | Mean | Maximum | Share of the processor |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Hard, earliest-deadline-first (as shipped) | 10,061 | **3.2 µs** | **8 µs** | **0.33 %** |
| Hard, deadline-monotonic | 10,060 | 3.2 µs | 8 µs | 0.33 % |
| Soft | 10,061 | 4.4 µs | 13 µs | 0.44 % |
| Power-aware | 10,060 | 4.2 µs | 9 µs | 0.43 % |

The 3.2 µs of 2026-09-23 is some 400 cycles per round at 125 MHz for the hard kernel,
under either algorithm: on four tasks, ordering the ready queue by deadline costs
nothing measurable. At `-O0` the hard kernel took 7.0 µs on average and 26 µs at worst,
so these numbers depend on the build as much as on the design. The soft kernel could not
be built for the Pico until 2026-09-22, because `TaskLEDPico` lacked its branch and the
`Makefile` never linked `EscapementSoft.o`.

Since 2026-09-25 every firmware is built with `-fno-strict-aliasing` (`method.md`). From
then until 2026-10-10, the checks read 3.4 to 3.5 µs a round on average, and 11 to 12 µs
at worst. They read 3.5 µs and 12 µs on 2026-10-04, and again on 2e30970 on 2026-10-10
(`method.md`, "What the README claims"). The bench has posted its log on each pull
request since 2026-10-13 (#29). Every log since then reads 4.4 µs on average and 11 µs
at worst. What raised the mean between 2026-10-10 and 2026-10-13 is not known yet (#47).

`tools/measure_cost.sh` counts the rounds from the load to the reading. That interval is
a little over 10 s. Its length depends on the machine that drives the probe
(`tools/board_ci.sh`): the board checks have read 10,067, 10,135 and 10,255 rounds. The
share of the processor divides the summed cost by 10 s, so it runs high by the same
margin.

The first publication also gave 3.2 and 8 µs, but with 13,409 rounds in 10 s and 0.43 %
of the processor. Rebuilt from its revision (7c84eca), the image counted 10,061. Counts
of some 13,500 came back on 2026-09-25: OpenOCD waited 3.3 s on each connection for the
Pico's own USB, which its firmware in flash enumerated. Selecting the probe by its ids
gave 10,140 again, and the tools now do so. This cause likely explains the first
publication too, but that is not established.

The power-aware kernel always runs its rounds at 125 MHz. Its idle task sleeps at full
speed (see below), so the alarm finds the core at full speed. The round lowers the speed
only after the next deadline is armed. In the build with the trace, the trace points add
about a microsecond. That trace gives 5.3 µs on average and 11 µs at worst over 276
rounds, all begun at 125 MHz.

The instrumentation lives in `Escapement_Timer.c` under
`ESCAPEMENT_MEASURE_SCHEDULING_COST`. It is **off in the example as shipped**. It adds
work to the critical path it measures. It also changes how the board behaves under a
debugger: the measurement build clears `TIMER_DBGPAUSE` (see “The timer and the
debugger” below). To reproduce the figures, uncomment the option in
`Escapement_Config.h`, rebuild, and run `tools/measure_cost.sh`. The script refuses a
binary that carries no counters.

The counters must be read one symbol at a time. The linker orders them differently from
one optimisation level to the next, so reading four consecutive words returns whatever
sits there. Here that was neighbouring pointers, printed as microseconds.

## Response times against their analysis

ZottaOS's manual bounds the response time of each task under fixed priorities, with the
kernel's costs included (User Manual, May 2012, eq. 2.3, p. 15). Each release costs
C_isw + C_timer + C_rsw: saving the running context, the timer's handler, restoring a
context. Each preemption by a task of higher priority costs its execution time plus
C_rsw. The manual gave 78 and 63 cycles for C_isw and C_rsw on the MSP430.

On 2026-09-27 `TaskLEDPico` ran on the Pico at 42db832, built with `make TRACE=1` and
the counters on. It ran once under DM and once under EDF. `tools/read_trace.py --csv
--absolute` read the trace 80 times over some 40 s. The trace gave the kernel's origin
with the events, so each task's releases are known exactly: every period from
`TimeOrigin`. The four tasks are the 1 ms probe, which marks only its start, and three
tasks of 10, 20 and 60 ms. Those three tasks mark their start and end on GPIO 25, 2 and
3.

The costs, read from the same trace, are these:

- from the alarm's interrupt, which fired on the release itself, to the probe's first
  instruction: 7 µs in the median under DM, 8 µs under EDF, 16 µs at most. That span is
  C_isw + C_timer + C_rsw together, and 16 µs is taken for each;
- from the probe's mark to the start of the 10 ms task after it: 1 to 2 µs. That span
  includes the probe's own work, its end and the switch. The probe's execution time is
  taken as 2 µs;
- the 10 and 20 ms tasks ran 40 to 41 and 80 to 81 µs from mark to mark, for 500 and
  1,000 turns of their loop: 0.08 µs a turn. The 60 ms task adds a turn at each
  instance, up to 4,000, over four minutes. The first traces saw it at 6 to 49 µs only.
  Its worst case, computed from 0.08 µs a turn, came to 321 µs. A second trace under DM,
  read from 200 to 245 s after the start, saw it at 4,000 turns: 384 µs. The estimate
  was low. The loop of the 60 ms task also reads `k`, a `volatile`, at every turn: 0.096
  µs a turn. The same trace under EDF saw 385 µs.

`tools/response_times.py` sets the bound beside the responses seen. Each response seen
is the end mark less its release:

| Task | Period = deadline | C | R, eq. 2.3 | R seen, DM | R seen, EDF |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| probe, GPIO 4 | 1 ms | 2 µs | 66 µs | no end mark | no end mark |
| GPIO 25 | 10 ms | 41 µs | 123 µs | 58 µs (1,510) | 58 µs (1,497) |
| GPIO 2 | 20 ms | 81 µs | 220 µs | 140 µs (773) | 141 µs (760) |
| GPIO 3 | 60 ms | 384 µs | 620 µs, 621 with C = 385 | 526 µs (260) | 527 µs (252) |

The figures in brackets are the instances seen in the second traces, read from 200 to
245 s after the start. No response passed its bound. The bound is loose for two reasons.
The equation counts one entry into the timer for every task released, but the four tasks
here are released at the same instant every 60 ms and share one entry. The equation also
charges the worst entry, 16 µs, to every release.

## Confirmed by an independent instrument

The frequency counter of a Bus Pirate v4, connected to `AUX` with a common ground,
measured the three outputs:

| Output | Task | Expected | Measured | Deviation |
|---|---|---:|---:|---:|
| `GP4` | 1 ms, toggles on every instance | 500.000 Hz | **500.02 Hz** | +40 ppm |
| `GP2` | 20 ms, pulse | 50.0000 Hz | **50.0014 Hz** | +28 ppm |
| `GP3` | 60 ms, pulse of varying width | 16.66667 Hz | **16.66713 Hz** | +28 ppm |

Eight consecutive readings on `GP3` agree to one part in a hundred thousand. The +28 ppm
deviation is **the same on the two most precise measurements**, so it is not noise. A
Pico 2 read the same on 2026-09-29: the cause is the instrument's reference, some 30 ppm
slow, not the board's crystal ("The Pico 2 on the board").

No software of the project takes part in this measurement. A second device confirms the
three scheduling periods. They include the period of the task whose execution time
varies from one instance to the next: that period does not vary.

## The timer and the debugger

The RP2040 freezes its counter as soon as the debugger halts a core (`TIMER_DBGPAUSE`).
The port **keeps that behaviour in the shipped build and turns it off in the measurement
build** (`ESCAPEMENT_MEASURE_SCHEDULING_COST`). The comment in `_OSInitializeTimer`
gives the reason for each.

When the pause is kept, time stops whenever the debugger stops a core, and stepping
through a real-time kernel needs that. Without the pause, the kernel clock ran on
through the halts of a load over SWD and through every inspection, while the tasks stood
still. The halts of a load are the reset, the image transfer and the restart of the
second core, several tens of milliseconds in all. A task of 10 ms or more absorbs that.
A task of 1 ms is twenty periods late. The catch-up loop makes it arrive again before it
has had a chance to run, so the overload guard fires, correctly. The state captured when
the guard fired showed three tasks late by 18 to 26 ms at the same instant. That is the
duration of the loading sequence.

When the pause is turned off, a running board can be watched. Otherwise three behaviours
of the debugger stand in the way. Each one was met on this board:

- **OpenOCD holds core 1.** It halts both cores to load an image and resumes core 0
  only. With core 1 held and the pause kept, the timer stays frozen. An alarm of the
  RP2040 fires on equality, so a deadline the counter has passed once it resumes is
  never reached again: the outputs still move, but no deadline comes. Reading the cost
  counters or the trace therefore takes the measurement build.
- **Core 1 held also pauses the watchdog** that a firmware in flash may have armed. A
  reset of the processors leaves the watchdog running. Once core 1 runs, the watchdog
  reboots the chip into the flash within a second (`WATCHDOG_REASON` = timer, found on
  2026-09-24). An image that runs core 1 is loaded with `set USE_CORE 0` and disarms the
  watchdog, as `FourSlotCoresPico` and `OSInitSleepGate` do.
- **Halting core 0 in the measurement build** lets time run on while the tasks stand
  still. The 1 ms probe misses its deadline, and the kernel stops on its overload guard.
  This stop was first taken for a defect of the kernel. The board is read without
  halting it, through the trace below.

## Two pitfalls met along the way

The first pitfall is reloading without a reset. A firmware loaded over SWD on top of
another finds the peripherals as the previous one left them. The timer was still armed
and its interrupt fired. `_OSIOHandler` found no descriptor and went into its trap with
interrupts disabled. That is why `reset halt` comes before `load_image`. Since
2026-10-05 the stack guard adds a reason: without the reset, the previous image's region
of the MPU stays enabled. The next image then faults in its own reset handler if its
globals reach that region.

The second pitfall is priming the transmission. On the USART of an STM32, `TXE` reflects
a state, so arming its interrupt fires it at once. On the PL011 of the RP2040, the
interrupt fires when the FIFO crosses a threshold. Arming that interrupt while the FIFO
is already empty produces nothing. `OSEnqueueUART` sets the UART's interrupt pending,
and the interrupt writes the first bytes. Until 2026-09-29 `OSEnqueueUART` wrote them
itself, with only the UART's interrupt masked. A task that preempted it and sent in turn
broke that scheme (`method.md`).

## The power-aware kernel on the board, through a trace

Watching a running board from the debugger disturbs what it watches ("The timer and the
debugger"). Each read of memory over SWD takes about a millisecond, so a series of reads
is not a snapshot. The port therefore has a trace
(`Escapement/CORTEX-Mx/Escapement_Trace.h`, `make TRACE=1`). The trace writes the
scheduling events, stamped with the timer, into a ring buffer of 256 entries.
`tools/read_trace.py` suspends the recording, reads the buffer in one transfer and lets
the recording go on, without stopping a core. The image is also built with
`ESCAPEMENT_MEASURE_SCHEDULING_COST`, so the kernel frees the timer from the debugger
itself, and the image runs untouched from the load on.

The trace of the hard kernel shows one round of every millisecond:

```
 40000 us  alarm0
 40001 us  soft>                       software timer handler entered
 40005 us  set_timer ahead 996 us      next deadline armed
 40006 us  soft<
 40007 us  mark gpio 4 start           the probe task runs
```

The first image of the power-aware kernel stalled at once. The trace showed the alarm
and the handler entered, then nothing. A reset of the processors alone does not reset
the timer, and the timer still had the previous image's alarm armed. The NVIC kept that
interrupt pending although it was not yet enabled. The NVIC took the interrupt as soon
as it was enabled, before the idle task had set the origin of time. The port now clears
the pending state. With that fix, the DVFS driver works on the silicon:

```
 31999 us  alarm0
 32000 us  soft>
 32004 us  set_timer ahead 995 us
 32007 us  speed 125 MHz -> 12 MHz     slowed down for the probe
 32016 us  soft<                        9 us: the handler, now at 12 MHz
 32029 us  mark gpio 4 start
 32047 us  speed 12 MHz -> 125 MHz     back to full speed in the idle task
```

The other tasks take the 50 MHz point. The figure in the README draws such a trace, from
`tools/read_trace.py --csv` through `tools/dvfs_figure.py`. The trace also shows that
the idle task returns to 125 MHz before it sleeps (`_OSSleep` of the power-aware
kernel), so the core spends nearly all its time asleep with the PLL at full speed. That
costs: 18.87 mA in WFI at 125 MHz against 5.09 at 12 (`power-aware.md`, measured on
2026-10-10).

### The other power-management policies

`make KERNEL=PA POWER=DRA`, `DR_OTE` or `DM_SLACK` builds `TaskLEDPico` under the three
policies other than OTE. Each policy ran on the board on 2026-09-24, first with the
trace and then with the cost counters of the section above (about 10 s per build, at
`-O2`):

| Policy | Speed changes in 6 × 256 events | Rounds (about 10 s) | Mean | Maximum | Share |
|---|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| OTE (default) | 12 ↔ 125 MHz about 190 times each way, 125 ↔ 50 MHz 18 | 10,058 | 4.2 µs | 9 µs | 0.43 % |
| DRA | none | 10,058 | 7.4 µs | 15 µs | 0.74 % |
| DR_OTE | as OTE | 10,058 | 6.5 µs | 14 µs | 0.65 % |
| DM_SLACK | as OTE | 10,059 | 4.2 µs | 9 µs | 0.43 % |

Every task keeps its period under all four policies. The probe runs every 1000 µs within
±20 µs, which is the jitter of the rounds at 12 MHz. The 10 and 20 ms tasks run within
±6 µs. DRA never changes speed, as under Renode. DRA gives a task only the time left by
instances of earlier deadline that have ended, and in this task set none is left. DR_OTE
and DM_SLACK pick the same speeds as OTE. DM_SLACK had already done so in every run on
the host before `reclaim`.

The price shows in the round. DRA and DR_OTE keep a second queue: a simulation of the
schedule at the worst-case execution times. Every arrival and every completion updates
that queue, and a round costs DRA and DR_OTE 2 to 3 µs more. DM_SLACK costs what OTE
costs. OTE was remeasured on the images these changes produced. It gives the 4.2 µs and
9 µs of 2026-09-23 again, and 5.3 µs and 11 µs through the trace.

## Timer events on the board

`TestTimerEventPico` leaves a mark in the trace each time one of its tasks raises or
lowers its output. The event manager leaves a mark when it delivers an event, with how
late the alarm fired. `tools/read_trace.py --pins` sums the marks up per output.
`tools/timer_events.py` loads the image and sums up several readings, as the checks on
the board do.

Two defects of the port stood in the way on 2026-09-22. The vector table sent alarms 2
and 3 to the handler of undefined interrupts. The port also wrote `INTE` and `INTF`
whole, which would have cleared another alarm's bits. The port now goes through the
atomic set and clear aliases. Five seconds of each kernel on 2026-09-23 gave:

| Kernel | GPIO 2: period, high | GPIO 3: period, high |
|---|---|---|
| Hard | 4999–5001 µs, 1012 µs | 10000 µs, 2012 µs |
| Soft | 4998–5002 µs, 1011–1012 µs | 10000 µs, 2012 µs |
| Power-aware | 4999–5001 µs, 1012–1013 µs | 10000 µs, 2013 µs |

Every event was delivered on the microsecond its alarm was set for. The 12 µs over the
requested 1000 and 2000 are the path from the alarm to the event-driven task: the
interrupt, the software timer handler and the context switch. The power-aware kernel
changed no speed, since event-driven tasks keep it at full speed by design.

On `GP3`, the Bus Pirate's frequency counter read 100.0031 Hz eight times in a row under
each of the three kernels, against 100 Hz expected. That is +31 ppm, against the +28
read on 2026-09-20: the cause is the counter's reference again, a few ppm apart. The
reading is the same to the last digit whichever kernel schedules the task and the event
that shape the output.

## Where the idle task sleeps

The power-aware kernel lets its idle task sleep at 125 MHz: `_OSSleep` raises the speed
before each `WFI`, and the core spends nearly all its time there. `make KERNEL=PA
SLEEP_SPEED=n` chooses another operating point (0 for 12 MHz, 1 for 50 MHz). The default
is unchanged, and so are its images. When the idle task sleeps slower, it raises a flag
before its `WFI`. The interrupt that wakes it raises the speed to the maximum as it
enters (`_OSIOHandler`). Only the idle task is sped up this way. The kernel counts the
work of an interrupted task at the speed it ran at, and a task running at 50 MHz must
not be counted at 125.

The figures below were measured on the board on 2026-09-23 with `TaskLEDPico` and
`TestTimerEventPico`. The cost of a round comes from the counters of `measure_cost.sh`,
which start once the speed is up. The rest comes from the trace:

| Idle sleeps at | Cost of a round | Alarm to probe | High time of the 1 ms event | Event late by |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 125 MHz (default) | 4.2 µs | 30 µs | 1012 µs | 0 µs |
| 12 MHz, speed raised by the kernel's handler | 21.3 µs | 57 µs | 1084 µs | 7 µs |
| 12 MHz, speed raised as the interrupt enters | 4.2 µs | 46 µs | 1028 µs | 16 µs |
| 12 MHz, the same through `_OSRaiseSpeedOnWake` | 4.2 µs | 42 µs | 1025 µs | 13 µs |
| 50 MHz, speed raised as the interrupt enters | 4.2 µs | | 1019 µs | 7 µs |

Every deadline holds, and the periods do not move, because the delay is the same at
every round. When the kernel's handler raised the speed, these steps had all run at 12
MHz first:

- the entry of the interrupt;
- the alarm handler;
- the switch to the software timer;
- the first part of the kernel.

When the speed is raised as the interrupt enters, only the entry itself runs slow, some
7 µs. The change of speed follows, some 9 µs from 12 to 125 MHz. That is more than a
switch of clock source should take, since the PLL stays locked. Sleeping at 12 MHz saves
far more than those microseconds cost: 6.60 mA against 19.75 for `TaskLEDPico`'s task
set under OTE (`power-aware.md`, 2026-10-10).

An audit of the ports on 2026-09-25 found a race in the idle loop. An interrupt between
the flag and the `WFI` raised the speed and cleared the flag. The idle task then slept
at 125 MHz until the next interrupt. When sleeping slower, the idle task now sets the
speed and the flag and executes `WFI` with interrupts masked. `WFI` still wakes on the
interrupt that becomes pending, and that interrupt is taken once interrupts are
unmasked. The measurement then gave, at 12 MHz: 1027 µs high, events 14 µs late and 4.5
µs a round. The default build was unchanged byte for byte. Whether the race cost
measurable energy was not measured.

`BenchDVFSPico.c` (`make KERNEL=PA bench`) times the changes of speed alone, without the
kernel. It times each change 10,000 times, on the 1 µs counter of the timer. At 12 MHz
the core and the counter run off the same crystal. A loop of fixed length would
therefore meet the counter at the same phase every time, and the mean would stay a whole
number of microseconds. A pseudo-random wait before each measurement breaks that
lock-step. `tools/dvfs_bench.py` loads the bench and prints the means.

The means below, of 2026-09-24, most likely include the two reads of the counter that
bracket each change. The bench was rebuilt later that day with another compiler on the
Mac mini. It then gave 8.83 µs from 12 to 125 MHz with the reads and 8.16 µs without.
The two reads cost 0.5 to 0.7 µs at 12 MHz and 0.06 µs at 125.

| Change of speed | Time |
|---|---:|
| 12 → 125 MHz | 8.7 µs |
| 12 → 50 MHz | 7.9 µs |
| 50 → 125 MHz | 5.5 µs |
| 125 → 50 MHz | 5.1 µs |
| 125 → 12 MHz | 4.1 µs |
| 50 → 12 MHz | 3.9 µs |

The register accesses of 12 → 125 MHz were timed one by one at 12 MHz. They add up to
some 2.6 µs:

- writing `VREG`: 0.4;
- parking `clk_sys` on the reference, where it already is: 1.0;
- the post-divider of the PLL: 0.5;
- `clk_sys` onto the PLL with its acknowledgement: 0.7.

No wait for the PLL or the regulator hides in there. The rest is the driver's own code
run at 12 MHz, where each instruction takes 83 ns: saving and masking interrupts, the
tests of the voltage table, the calls.

The wake-up therefore has a path of its own, `_OSRaiseSpeedOnWake`. `_OSIOHandler` takes
that path when the idle task's flag is up. The path makes only the tests the way up
needs. From 12 MHz it does not park `clk_sys` on the reference it is already on. It
tests and clears the flag with interrupts masked, so that an interrupt of higher
priority arriving meanwhile raises the speed itself. The path takes 6.0 µs from 12 MHz,
where `OSSetProcessorSpeed` took 8.7. It takes 4.3 µs from 50 MHz instead of 5.5. The
probe then starts 42 µs after its alarm, and the event-driven task runs 13 µs late: that
is the fourth row of the table above.

### SLEEP rather than WFI alone

`make SLEEP_GATE=1` builds `TaskLEDPico` and `TestTimerEventPico` with `OSInitSleepGate`
(`Escapement_SleepGate.c`). The idle task's `WFI` then puts the chip in SLEEP, where
every clock is gated but the timer's. The tick comes from clk_ref, which SLEEP does not
gate. SLEEP needs both cores asleep with deep sleep enabled, so `OSInitSleepGate` parks
core 1 in `WFE` for good. The CI does not build this variant.

On the board on 2026-09-28, `TestTimerEventPico` of the hard kernel ran in the cost
build with `TRACE=1`, read by `timer_events.py`. It gave the same figures with and
without the gate:

- periods of 4999 to 5001 µs and 10000 µs;
- high times of 1010 to 1012 µs and 2011 to 2012 µs;
- 147 to 150 events, each 0 µs late.

Both cores read asleep with deep sleep set (`S_SLEEP` of DHCSR, `SLEEPDEEP` of SCR).
Only one witness shows that the chip is in SLEEP: the probe's reads below. No current
was measured here. On the Pico 2, SLEEP with PLL_SYS stopped took 82 % off the idle
task's current (issue #6). Here the PLLs kept running, until the next subsection.

The bench showed three things that the datasheet does not say:

- **The probe reads zeros while the chip sleeps.** With `SLEEP_EN0` at 0, the bus fabric
  and the SRAM have no clock. Every read of the bus returns 0 without an error,
  `CHIP_ID` included, but DHCSR, inside the core, still reads. Holding core 1 with the
  debugger takes the chip out of SLEEP and leaves core 0 and the kernel running.
  `read_trace.py` does that for an image with `OSInitSleepGate`. Holding core 0 for a
  read of the trace instead stopped the kernel on its overload guard, in the cost build
  whose timer runs on.
- **`SLEEP_EN0` and `SLEEP_EN1` outlive a debugger's reset, and the rescue DP's.** An
  image without the gate, loaded after an image with it, found them as the image with
  the gate had left them. `OSInitializeSystemClocks` now sets them back to their values
  at power-on.
- **Core 1 must be released after core 0 starts the image.** Left held, as the tools
  otherwise leave it, core 1 keeps `OSLaunchCore1` waiting for its bootrom.
  `read_trace.load` resumes core 1 after core 0 for an image with the gate. OpenOCD has
  no `rp2040.core0 resume`: the commands are `targets rp2040.core1` then `resume`. With
  core 1 running, the watchdog of the firmware in flash rebooted the chip ("The timer
  and the debugger"), so `OSInitSleepGate` disarms it. `OSInitSleepGate` also launches
  core 1 before it sets the gates. On the RP2350, with the gates set first, core 0 slept
  while it waited for core 1's bootrom on the inter-core FIFO, whose clock was gated
  (2026-10-04).

The tick generator does not need `CLK_SYS_WATCHDOG`, the clock of the watchdog's bus
interface. The first version kept that clock. With it gated, on 2026-09-28, the timer
counted 5.1178 and 5.1168 s while the UNO Q's clock counted 5.1177 and 5.1174 s. Core 1
was held for each of the two reads. The version that kept the clock gave 5.1198 s for
5.1201, and the image without the gate 5.1119 s for 5.1104. `timer_events.py` gave the
same figures as above: 148 and 149 events, each 0 µs late. The mask now keeps
`CLK_SYS_TIMER` alone.

### SLEEP with PLL_SYS stopped

Since 2026-10-10 (roadmap, task 10), the idle task built with `SLEEP_GATE=1` stops
PLL_SYS for each sleep long enough, as the RP2350's idle task does. `GatedSleep` finds
the first alarm that the kernel or the timer events have armed (`_OSAlarmTime`, which
the timer and the events write as they arm one). If that alarm is 300 µs away or more,
`GatedSleep` arms alarm 3 to end the sleep 150 µs before it. It then puts clk_sys on the
crystal through clk_ref. It moves the auxiliary source off PLL_SYS, as `SleepPico`
showed must be done, and powers PLL_SYS down. On waking, it powers PLL_SYS up and waits
for its lock, some 55 µs. It takes clk_sys back at the operating point in effect. It
then waits in SLEEP, with PLL_SYS running, for the alarm the kernel set. A shorter sleep
keeps PLL_SYS running. Interrupts stay masked throughout, so an interrupt of another
source, such as a UART's, is taken late by the lock. Alarm 3 belongs to the sleep: timer
events given alarm 3 first leave PLL_SYS running, and `OSInitTimerEvent` refuses alarm 3
after `OSInitSleepGate`. The RP2350 port got the same guard.

On probe2's Pico on 2026-10-10, powered by the PPK2 at 3.3 V, `TestTimerEventPico` was
built as above. It gave the figures of the table in "Timer events on the board":

- under the hard kernel, periods of 4999 to 5001 µs and 10000 µs, high times of 1011 to
  1012 µs and 2011 µs, and 237 events, each 0 µs late;
- under the power-aware kernel, high times of 1012 to 1013 µs and 2012 µs, and 238
  events, each 0 µs late.

`TaskLEDPico` ran 60 s in each case, with its LED moved to GP18, off the board:

| Kernel and policy | SLEEP, PLL_SYS running | SLEEP, PLL_SYS stopped |
|---|---:|---:|
| hard, racing | 5.49 mA | 2.18 mA (−60 %) |
| power-aware, OTE | 5.53 mA | 2.22 mA (−60 %) |
| power-aware, DR_OTE | 5.61 mA | 2.41 mA (−57 %) |

`SleepPico`, at 1.22 mA, wakes ten times a second, but `TaskLEDPico`'s probe task wakes
every millisecond. Each wake-up spends the lock awake and the 150 µs margin with PLL_SYS
running.

### The regulator

`BenchVregPico.c`, in the same target, times the core regulator. The chip's only witness
of the regulator's output is the `ROK` bit of `VREG`. That bit reports the output in
regulation (about 90 % of the target, by the usual reading). The bit does not drop for
the step the driver makes, 1.05 to 1.10 V. The bench therefore steps up from lower
voltages, with the core at 12 MHz and the brown-out detector lowered to 0.817 V, as
`UNDERVOLT=1` does. That is below the 1.05 V the datasheet guarantees, and the chip went
through it without a fault. The table gives the time until `ROK` is back, over 1,000
steps each, on 2026-09-24:

| Step | Low voltage held 500 µs first | Held 3 ms first |
|---|---:|---:|
| 0.90 → 1.10 V | 46.6 µs, 48 at most | 47.7 µs, 49 at most |
| 0.95 → 1.10 V | `ROK` never drops | 30.9 µs, 34 at most |
| 0.90 → 1.05 V | 26.3 µs, 27 at most | 26.5 µs, 27 at most |
| 1.00 → 1.10 V | `ROK` never drops | `ROK` never drops |
| 1.05 → 1.10 V | `ROK` never drops | `ROK` never drops |

On the way up, the regulator reports its output in regulation within 50 µs. When the
core is undervolted, the driver waits 100 µs (`RP2040_VREG_SETTLING_US`) before raising
the clock, which leaves a margin of two. `ROK` cannot say how long the last tenth takes.
On the way down the output is slow. After 500 µs at 0.95 V, it had not fallen far enough
for `ROK` to drop on the way back up: a linear regulator lightly loaded at 12 MHz has
only that load to pull its output down. A lower voltage requested is therefore not a
lower voltage obtained for hundreds of microseconds, which matters to what DVFS saves.

### For the PPK2: SleepPico

`SleepPico.c` (`make KERNEL=PA sleep`) is the RP2040's counterpart of the Pico 2's
`SleepPico2`. It was written on 2026-10-10 for the energy verdict (roadmap, task 9),
before the plain Pico and the PPK2 it needs were on the bench. It runs no kernel: it
uses the port's clocks and its DVFS driver. Each phase has the same load, some 100 µs of
work at 125 MHz every 100 ms. Core 1 stays in deep sleep for good. There are four phases
of 30 s. GP16 and GP17 show the phase number for the PPK2's D0 and D1, and a line goes
out on UART0 at each change. The phases are:

- WFI at 125 MHz;
- WFI at 12 MHz, with the work back at 125 on waking, as for the idle task built with
  `SLEEP_SPEED=0`;
- SLEEP at 125 MHz, every clock gated but the timer's;
- SLEEP with clk_sys on the crystal and PLL_SYS powered down, locked again on waking,
  with the lock timed.

Built with `RUN=1`, the core computes a CRC-32 without a pause, 30 s at each operating
point of the driver. Each result is checked against the first.

`SleepPico` ran on probe1's Pico W on 2026-10-10, without the PPK2, as a check. Every
phase took its 20 periods (`PHASE_PERIODS=20`). PLL_SYS locked again in 55 µs at most,
54.9 µs in the mean over 20 wake-ups. Under `RUN=1`, the work done in 30 s fell with the
clock: 176,289 CRCs at 125 MHz, 70,516 at 50 (0.400) and 16,924 at 12 (0.096). None was
wrong, under `UNDERVOLT=1` too, at 0.95 and 0.90 V. The first build took a HardFault
waking from SLEEP with PLL_SYS powered down, with the stacked PC just past the WFI. The
fault came although clk_sys ran from clk_ref: its auxiliary source still named PLL_SYS.
Awake, or in SLEEP with PLL_SYS running, the build did not fault. With the auxiliary
source on the crystal, it does not fault either. A kernel that stops PLL_SYS in its idle
task must do the same.

## The 4-slot buffer between the two cores

The kernel schedules on core 0 alone. Its lock-free mechanisms were checked on one core,
with an interrupt handler as the writer. Of the three mechanisms, Simpson's four slots
need no atomic instruction. They were designed for a writer and a reader on separate
processors, so they should hold between the two cores of the RP2040 as they stand. The
3-slot buffer and the FIFO queue should not. Their LL/SC pair is emulated with a
reservation bit that interrupts clear and a store from the other core does not. The
announced operation of the queue also assumes preemptions that nest.

The model came first. `test/model/fourslot.py` also explores two cores. The writer runs
beside the reader and publishes a slot in four steps, one per statement of
`OSWriteBuffer`. Any of the reader's steps can fall in between. The kernel's buffer
holds. A writer that publishes the pair before its slot passes on one core but is caught
on two.

Then came the board. `FourSlotCoresPico` starts core 1 with `OSLaunchCore1`
(`Escapement_Core1.c`). That function resets core 1 through the power-on state machine.
It then runs the bootrom's handshake over the inter-core FIFO, as the pico-sdk does.
Core 1 runs bare. It writes records of eight words, all eight the same counter rising by
one, as fast as it can. It writes them into a 4-slot buffer and into a plain array. A
task on core 0 reads both 32 times each millisecond. The task counts the reads that mix
two records and those that go backwards. `tools/fourslot_cores.sh` loads the example and
reads the counts back while both cores run. On 2026-09-24, 10 s of each kernel gave:

| Kernel | Records written | Reads of each | 4-slot: torn, backwards | Plain array: torn |
|---|---:|---:|---|---:|
| Hard, EDF | 1,260,703 | 321,760 | **0, 0** | 17,061 (5.3 %) |
| Hard, DM | 1,260,817 | 321,760 | **0, 0** | 17,192 |
| Soft | 1,261,074 | 321,792 | **0, 0** | 17,213 |
| Power-aware, reader every 1 ms | 1,260,782 | 321,760 | **0, 0** | 16,815 |
| Power-aware, reader every 2 ms | 1,047,620 | 160,896 | **0, 0** | 3,269 |

No read of the buffer mixed two records or went back to an older one. The plain array,
with the same writer and reader but without the mechanism, tore about one read in
twenty. A run of the reader took 314 µs at most, with core 1 contending for the memory.
The reader was declared at 400 µs every millisecond. That left the power-aware kernel no
lower speed to take, and it stayed at 125 MHz (trace). The example now runs the reader
every 2 ms under that kernel. With that period, the kernel takes the reader down to 50
MHz and the idle task back to 125, 86 times each way in two readings of the trace. Core
1 shares the clock and writes through both speeds. It writes a sixth fewer records than
at 125 MHz throughout. The buffer still gives no torn read and none going backwards.

These runs predate the memory barriers of 2026-09-25. The model can let the cores
perform their loads and stores out of program order, as Armv6-M allows (A3.7). Once it
does, the buffer needs four barriers, which the kernels now have (`method.md`). The
board had shown no torn read without the barriers, but the architecture did not promise
it. With the barriers, the checks on the board below read 326,525 reads under the hard
kernel and 163,424 under the power-aware one on 2026-09-25 (6f90fa2). None was torn or
went backwards. The plain array tore 3,428 and 1,675 times, about 1 %. In the runs
above, without the barriers, it had torn 5 % under the hard kernel and 2 % under the
power-aware one. Why it tore less is not established.

Two things stood in the way:

- OpenOCD holds core 1, which then cannot be started. Running core 1 woke the watchdog
  of the firmware in flash ("The timer and the debugger"). The script has OpenOCD handle
  core 0 only (`set USE_CORE 0`), and the example disarms the watchdog.
- The two loops fell into step. Both cores run off the same clock and loop without end.
  Without a random wait before each read, 6528 reads of the plain array in a row missed
  every write to it, and the negative control proved nothing. The reader now dithers.

The 3-slot buffer and a FIFO queue across the cores need the exclusive accesses of the
Pico 2's Cortex-M33. `ThreeSlotCoresPico2` and `FIFOCoresPico2` run them there, under
Renode and on a Pico 2 (`architecture.md`).

## The endurance test

`SoakPico` runs everything the kernel offers at once, and each part checks itself
(`SoakPico.c`, its header comment):

- a pulse of 1 ms timed on the kernel's clock;
- the FIFO queue with three producers (a long task, a short one preempting it, and an
  interrupt of alarm 3 at pseudo-random intervals) and an event-driven task emptying it;
- a 3-slot buffer, read once each time;
- timer events;
- a 4-slot buffer written by core 1;
- a heartbeat that feeds the watchdog;
- the stacks and the guard words.

The long task's load comes in phases of 5 to 60 s, drawn at random. Each phase has a
load drawn between about a fifth and three quarters of the processor. Until 2026-09-26,
and in the runs below, the load went to three quarters for 20 s of every minute.

`tools/soak.py pico DURATION INTERVAL` loads the firmware into SRAM and reads its counts
over SWD without stopping it. The watchdog block records the reason for each restart.
The script logs a restart and counts it as a failure, then loads the image again, and
the test goes on. Given a token, the script posts the commit status `board/soak`. While
the script runs, it holds the lock of its Debug Probe, and the board CI checks only the
boards wired to the other probes. The script keeps the state of the run beside its log.
When it is started again after its machine rebooted, it continues the run if the image
ran on meanwhile. Otherwise it loads the image again and counts an interruption, apart
from the board's restarts. This was tried on 2026-09-28 on the bench's Pico. The script,
stopped for 19 s, continued an image whose count had gone from 31 to 68 s. An image
replaced meanwhile by another image counted an interruption.

The README's badge shows, through shields.io, the latest status of the commit that the
tag `soak-pico` names, which is the commit under test. shields.io refuses a filter on
the context, so the badge shows whatever context was posted last on that commit. The
endurance test posts when its restarts, errors or interruptions change, or an hour after
its last post, since GitHub keeps at most 1,000 statuses per commit and context
(34d86a4). A new run moves the tag to its own commit, its `BOARD_SOAK_SHA`: `git tag -f
soak-pico SHA && git push -f origin soak-pico`.

The bench's Pico gave these results under the hard kernel and EDF, on 2026-09-25:

- a first firmware, without the interrupt or the load phases, ran 30 min with no error.
  It crossed one wrap of the kernel clock. The pulse was at most 76 µs late, and the
  timer events at most 65;
- with the interrupt and the phases, before the day's fixes, the buffer gave slots twice
  from the first minute (31 in 61 s, 56 in 121 s). The board restarted once, and the
  test caught the restart and went on (`method.md` describes the two fixes). The
  watchdog reset the chip only because the firmware in flash had set `PSM_WDSEL`, which
  the example did not: the example has set it itself since 2026-09-30 (a review of the
  port);
- the same firmware after the fixes ran 10 min with no restart and no error. It took
  1.38 million interrupts. The pulse was at most 103 µs late, and the timer events at
  most 117. At least 952 bytes of core 1's stack were never used.

The two weeks that followed, on a board of its own, began on 2026-09-28 (`roadmap.md`,
item 0). The first week ran under the hard kernel at 69825e4. It ended on 2026-10-05
with no error and no restart in 7 days and 563 wraps. The second week ran under the
power-aware kernel at 80cc6dc. It began the same evening and was stopped on 2026-10-10
after 4 d 18 h 47 min, for the PPK2 of the energy verdict. It had no error and no
restart in 384 wraps. The counts are 32-bit. The queue's count, some 4,000 a second,
wraps after 12.5 days. `tools/soak.py` therefore compares two readings modulo 2^32, as a
review of the U5 port pointed out (2026-09-25).

## The Pico from its flash

`make FLASH=1` links the examples into the flash, in `build-flash/`, after a second
stage of the boot of our own (`Escapement_Boot2.S`, `RP2040_FLASH.ld`). The bootrom
checks the CRC of the second stage, which `tools/rp2040_boot2.py` appends, and runs the
second stage from SRAM. The second stage sets the QSPI pads as the pico-sdk's does. It
sets the Quad Enable bit of the W25Q16JV as a volatile bit. It has the XIP read with the
Fast Read Quad Output, 6Bh, at clk_sys / 4: the Fast Read Quad I/O of the pico-sdk's
boot2 runs in a continuous mode that the W25Q16JV's datasheet does not describe.
`tools/pico_flash.sh` writes an image and boots it. `fourslot_cores.sh`,
`measure_cost.sh`, `timer_events.py` and `dvfs_bench.py` do so by themselves for an
image linked into the flash. The flash of the bench's Pico held an image of the
pico-sdk's, 45 sectors, kept on the UNO Q (`~/pico-flash-before-2026-10-09.bin`).

On 2026-10-09, writing and booting first left the flash mute after two or three images
in a row. Its JEDEC ID read 0xffffff. It answered neither a release from power-down
(ABh) nor a reset (66h, 99h). A reset of the whole RP2040 through the rescue port did
not help. Only powering the Pico off and on did, three times. Those images were written
with clk_sys at 125 MHz, the SSI at 31 MHz from the second stage, and the pads at their
reset values, 4 mA and a slow slew. Then the pads were set, and the chip was first reset
through the rescue port, so that the image is written with the clocks back on the ring
oscillator. With these two changes, 30 images in a row were written and verified. Which
of the two changes did it is not known.

The checks from the flash gave, the same day:

| Check | From the flash | From SRAM (CI) |
|---|---|---|
| `FourSlotCoresPico`, hard kernel | 324,640 reads, 0 torn; plain array torn 0 times | 323,456, 0; 3,238 |
| `FourSlotCoresPico`, power-aware | 31 reads, the kernel's guard stops it | 161,728, 0; 1,681 |
| the same, period 20 ms: run at 12, at 50 MHz | longest run 4,857 µs, 1,166 µs | |
| `TaskLEDPico`, a round's cost | mean 3.5 µs, maximum 280 µs; 13, kernel in SRAM | 3.5, 12 |
| `TestTimerEventPico`, three kernels | every event delivered 0 µs late | the same |
| `BenchDVFSPico`, 12 to 125 MHz | 8.85 µs | 8.84 |

There were three findings. The plain array is the witness that the two cores did
overlap. It never tore from the flash, as with `LitmusPico2`'s LB on the Pico 2 ("From
the flash" below): the two cores fetch through the one XIP cache. The criterion of the
plain array stays that of the images in SRAM, as the user decided on 2026-10-09: what it
witnesses does not depend on where the code is.

Under the power-aware kernel, the reader's first instance never ended: the kernel's
guard stopped the chip at its next arrival. A trace shows why. The first round of the
kernel took 856 µs to start the reader, 347 of them to set the timer and 116 to move to
50 MHz, with its code not yet in the XIP cache. At 50 MHz the reader needs some 1,166
µs, and its period is 2,000. Away from that first round, run time follows the frequency
from the flash as from SRAM. The reader's longest run, 466 µs at 125 MHz, was 4,857 µs
at 12 and 1,166 at 50, within 0.1 % of the ratio. A round's cost, 3.5 µs on average,
reaches 280 µs while the cache is cold. That cost was first taken here for a change of
speed stopping core 1 (8108a72), but core 1, the writer, ran on throughout. 466 µs is
also more than the 400 µs that the example declares for the reader, measured from SRAM.

The kernel and the port therefore run from SRAM in an image linked into the flash, as
the user decided on 2026-10-09. `RP2040_FLASH.ld` and `RP2350_FLASH.ld` place the code
and the constants of every `Escapement*.o` in `.data`. `_OSResetHandler` copies that
section, and `_OSResetHandler` itself stays in the flash with the application. Then,
from the flash:

- the power-aware `FourSlotCoresPico` read 161,504 times in 10 s, as in SRAM;
- a round of the kernel took some 7 µs, its first included;
- a round's cost was 13 µs at most, against 12 in SRAM;
- a change from 12 to 125 MHz took 9.51 µs.

The reader itself, still read through the XIP, took 411 µs at most at 125 MHz and 1,039
at 50: an image in the flash declares 520 µs for the reader, the margin the 400 kept
over 314 in SRAM. With that declaration, the example read 161,536 times. The Pico 2's
examples, with the kernel in SRAM too, passed `pico2_check.py --flash` and
`pico2_uart.py --flash` the same day. The STM32's flash is inside the chip, behind its
own cache and wait states, and it is left as it is.

`BenchDVFSPico` runs without the kernel. It set neither the timer nor its tick: it had
counted on those of the image before, which a reset of the whole chip clears. It now
sets both. An image loaded into SRAM after one booted from the flash finds core 1 still
running the flash's image. A reset of the cores leaves core 1 running, as on the Pico 2,
and only a rescue stops it. The Pico's flash again holds the pico-sdk's image it held,
so that the board checks, in SRAM, find what they found before.

| `GP2` | `TestTimerEventPico2`, 5 ms event | 200 Hz | 200.007 Hz, 4 |
| `GP3` | `TestTimerEventPico2`, 10 ms event | 100 Hz | 100.0031 Hz, 4 |

Every output is +28 to +35 ppm off, as the Pico's outputs were on the same instrument
("Confirmed by an independent instrument", 16.66713 and 100.0031 Hz). Two boards read
the same offset, so the cause is the Bus Pirate's reference, some 30 ppm slow, not
either crystal.

The UART was checked through the Debug Probe's own UART on GP0 and GP1, on 2026-09-30
(`tools/pico2_uart.py`, the CI's images of 3c141ca). `UARTEchoPico2` sent back the
suite's line, the 256 byte values and 1,408 bytes of lines sent back to back. Each byte
came back once and in order. `UARTSendersPico2` put 18,106 lines on the port in 20 s.
Every line was full, and none was broken. The tasks had queued 17,658 lines when their
counts were read. That count lay between the 17,553 lines on the port just before and
the 17,661 just after.

The 2^30 wrap was checked on the board the same day. `TaskWrapPico2` has its periods
scaled for Renode, a thousand times too long here, so `SoakPico2` crossed the wrap
instead, as the Pico's endurance test did ("The endurance test"). `SoakPico2` ran 2,400
s across two wraps and was read every minute over SWD without stopping a core
(`tools/pico2_soak.py`). It had no error in any of its eight parts and no restart. The
pulse was at most 46 µs late, and the timer events at most 40. At least 952 bytes of
core 1's stack were never used.

### From the flash

`make FLASH=1` links the same examples into the flash, in `build-flash/`
(`RP2350_FLASH.ld`). The RP2350's bootrom wants no boot2. It boots an image that has an
IMAGE_DEF block in its first 4 kB (RP2350 datasheet, 5.2.7 and 5.9.5.1). It enters the
image in the QSPI mode in which it found the flash readable, and the image keeps that
mode. `Escapement_ImageDef.S` holds that block, after the vector table. The block holds
the image type and an entry point, `_OSRamEntry`, so that ACTLR.EXTEXCLALL is set as it
is for an image in SRAM. `tools/pico2_flash.sh` writes an image after a rescue of the
chip and resets core 0, whose bootrom then boots the image. `pico2_check.py --flash` and
`pico2_uart.py --flash` hold the images booted this way to the criteria of the images in
SRAM. The flash of the bench's Pico 2 held 12 written sectors but nothing bootable, with
its first sector erased. A copy is on the UNO Q (`~/pico2-flash/before-2026-10-09.bin`).

On 2026-10-09, from the flash, both slot buffers, the queue between the cores,
`IPCPico2` and `SoakPico2` passed. The UART echo passed too. The senders passed once the
tool stopped the previous image before clearing the port: a rescue that cut a line had
left a byte 0 before the new image's first byte. `LitmusPico2` read no weak outcome in 2
million rounds of each test. But LB never showed its overlapping outcome, both loads
before both stores, twice in a row. With only its two halves moved into SRAM, MP lost
its own overlapping outcome and LB had 2,000. With `Wait` moved too, all three plain
tests lost theirs. The offsets that `Wait` reaches thus depend on where the code is
fetched from, not on the order of memory. The criterion is met from SRAM only. It stays
that of the images in SRAM, as the user decided on 2026-10-09: `pico2_check.py --flash`
prints the overlapping outcomes without requiring them.

A reset by the watchdog, as the pico-sdk's `watchdog_reboot` selects it in PSM_WDSEL,
booted an image whose core 0 the debugger could not reach afterwards. The access port of
core 0 answered WAIT for good. The reset of core 0 alone does not do this, and it is the
reset the tool uses.

## Checks on the board

The CI runs everything under emulation. The board runs on the machine it is wired to.
That machine pulls from GitHub rather than being pushed to. `tools/board_ci.sh` is
started by a timer. It fetches `main` and the open pull requests. For each commit not
yet checked, it runs seven checks on the Pico and posts the outcome as the commit status
`board/pico`. The CI builds the images for each commit (`tools/board_images.sh`,
artifact `board-images`), and the bench takes them (`BOARD_CI_IMAGES=ci`). A bench with
a compiler can build them itself. The bench once checked the compiled order under a
second compiler. The CI checks that order under two. The checks are:

- `FourSlotCoresPico`, hard kernel: no read of the 4-slot buffer torn or going backwards
  in 10 s, and the plain array torn at least once;
- the same under the power-aware kernel, which changes speed under both cores;
- `TaskLEDPico` with the cost counters: its 1 ms round, over 10 s, at most 5 µs on
  average;
- `TestTimerEventPico` under each of the three kernels, through its trace
  (`tools/timer_events.py`): both outputs on their periods within 5 µs, high for 1 and 2
  ms plus the path from the alarm to the task within 20 µs, and every event at most 2 µs
  after its alarm;
- `BenchDVFSPico` (`tools/dvfs_bench.py`): every change of speed and the wake-up path
  within a quarter over the means above, and the wake-up path shorter than the general
  one from the same speed, which is its purpose.

`BenchVregPico` stays out, because it takes the core below its specified voltage, and a
check run on every commit should not do that. The same bench also checks a Pico 2 and
posts `board/pico2`. It also checks the UNO Q's own STM32U5 and posts `board/u5`. It
also checks the STM32U3 of a NUCLEO-U385RG-Q plugged into it and posts `board/u3`
(`tools/board_ci.md`).

Only `main` and the pull requests from a branch of this repository are ever checked
there. Only the repository's owner and Dependabot push to those branches (since
2026-10-13, `tools/board_ci.md`). A self-hosted runner would have run pull requests from
forks too, on a machine of the home network with a probe on it. A public repository
cannot rule that out by configuration alone.

The first run was on 2026-09-24 against 110b3cc. It gave 321,760 reads under each
kernel, none torn, the plain array torn 17,057 and 16,814 times, and 10,069 rounds of
3.2 µs. The bench was then a Linux machine, and later a Mac mini. Since 2026-09-26 it
has been an Arduino UNO Q. The UNO Q's Linux drives the probe and the Pico through a
USB-C hub and takes the CI's images. Its first run, against 3b80541, gave 322,880 reads
of the 4-slot buffer under the hard kernel and 161,472 under the power-aware one, none
torn. The plain array tore 4,233 and 1,931 times. The round took 3.5 µs over 10,116
rounds. The timer events kept within 3 µs of their periods, with none late. The change
from 12 to 125 MHz took 8.83 µs.

`tools/board_ci.md` describes the installation: under systemd on Linux or on the UNO Q,
or under launchd on a Mac.
